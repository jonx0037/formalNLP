## Overview & Motivation

Ask a language model to continue "revenue". In our small example its first word is "rose" with probability $0.45$, "was" with $0.35$, or "fell" with $0.20$. Greedy decoding takes "rose", then the likeliest word after it, "sharply", and stops: the string has probability $0.0864$. But "was" is followed by "flat" four times in five, and "revenue was flat" ends the sentence with probability $0.238$, almost three times as likely. Greedy decoding never looked. A beam of width two keeps both "rose" and "was", finds "was flat", and can prove that nothing it dropped could have done better.

That example has the whole topic in it. A model gives every string a probability, and the most probable string, the **mode**, is what most decoders aim for. Finding it is a search problem, and we develop it in order:

1. **The mode is a shortest path.** With edge costs $-\log p$, uniform-cost search finds it exactly, and a Markov model's prefixes merge so that Dijkstra's algorithm finds it in polynomial time.
2. **Greedy and beam search are pruned versions of that search.** Neither has an approximation guarantee, and a wider beam can do worse.
3. **One extra number certifies success.** If the best string beats every candidate the beam pruned, it is the mode.
4. **The mode is biased toward stopping.** Normalizing by length fights the bias, but it breaks exact search, and without a length cap it can have no optimum.
5. **Greedy decoding can loop forever,** even on a model that ends every string with probability one.

Then we measure **search error**, missing the mode, against **model error**, the mode being a poor answer, on two models trained on the shared filings. Wider beams remove the first and expose the second.

## Decoding as a shortest path

We write $\mathrm{eos}$ for the end mark, the token `</s>` that closes every sentence.

<TheoremBlock type="definition" number={1} title="The decoding problem">

A language model over a vocabulary $V$ assigns a prompt $x$ and a string $y = (y_1, \dots, y_n)$ with $y_n = \mathrm{eos}$ the probability

$$
p(y \mid x) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{n} p(y_t \mid x, y_{\lt t}).
$$

The **mode** is $y^\star = \arg\max_y p(y \mid x)$. The model is **consistent** if these probabilities sum to $1$ over all finite strings.

</TheoremBlock>

A consistent model has a mode: only finitely many strings can have probability above any $\varepsilon \gt 0$, so the supremum is a maximum. The [neural-language-models topic](/topics/neural-language-models) proves that a softmax recurrent model with bounded state is consistent (its Theorem 4). Picture the strings as the leaves of a tree. The root is the prompt, each node is a prefix $h$, and each edge adds one token.

<TheoremBlock type="proposition" number={1} title="The mode is a shortest path">

Give the edge from $h$ to $hw$ the weight $c(h, w) = -\log p(w \mid x, h) \ge 0$. Then $-\log p(y \mid x)$ is the total weight of the path from the root to $y$, and the mode is the cheapest path from the root to an $\mathrm{eos}$ leaf.

</TheoremBlock>

<TheoremBlock type="proof">

Take $-\log$ of the product in Definition 1. Each factor is at most $1$, so each weight is non-negative.

</TheoremBlock>

The weights are surprisals, formalML's [information content](https://www.formalml.com/topics/shannon-entropy) (its Definition 1). Because they are non-negative, the cost of a prefix, $c(h) = -\log \pi(h)$ with $\pi(h)$ the probability that the string begins with $h$, can only grow as the prefix grows. That monotonicity is what every exact method on this page uses.

<TheoremBlock type="theorem" number={1} title="Uniform-cost search is exact">

Uniform-cost search keeps a frontier of prefixes, starting with the root, and repeatedly removes the cheapest one. If it ends in $\mathrm{eos}$ the search returns it; otherwise its children join the frontier. For a consistent model the search ends, and the string it returns is a mode. If moreover $\mathbb{E}|Y| \lt \infty$, where $|Y|$ counts the words before $\mathrm{eos}$, the search removes at most $(2 + \mathbb{E}|Y|)/p(y^\star)$ prefixes.

</TheoremBlock>

<TheoremBlock type="proof">

Every finished string not yet removed has a prefix on the frontier: the root is a prefix of every string, and when $h$ is removed its children take its place for every string through $h$. In particular, a mode $y^\star$ exists, and until it is removed one of its prefixes is on the frontier, at cost at most $c(y^\star)$. The search removes the cheapest prefix, so every prefix removed up to that point has $\pi(h) \ge p(y^\star)$.

Now the search ends. The open prefixes of length $t$ have total probability $\Pr(|Y| \ge t)$, so at most $\Pr(|Y| \ge t)/p(y^\star)$ of them are that probable. Consistency gives $\Pr(|Y| \ge t) \to 0$, so past some length no open prefix qualifies, and only finitely many do, even if $\mathbb{E}|Y|$ is infinite. At most $1/p(y^\star)$ finished strings qualify too. So the search removes finitely many prefixes before it removes a finished string.

Suppose the first finished string removed is $h^\star$, at cost $c$. Every frontier prefix then costs at least $c$, and every finished string $y$ not yet removed has a frontier prefix of cost at most $c(y)$, so $c(y) \ge c$ and $h^\star$ is a mode.

For the work, sum the count of open prefixes over $t \ge 0$. That gives $(1 + \mathbb{E}|Y|)/p(y^\star)$, since $\sum_{t \ge 0} \Pr(|Y| \ge t) = 1 + \mathbb{E}|Y|$, and the finished strings add at most $1/p(y^\star)$.

</TheoremBlock>

Consistency is doing real work there. Let the root go to $w$ with probability $0.9$ or $x$ with $0.1$, let $x$ end the string, and let $w$ repeat with probability $1$. The finished strings sum to $0.1$, and the mode is $x\,\mathrm{eos}$. The search removes $w$, $ww$, $www$, and so on, each of probability $0.9$, and never reaches it.

This is Dijkstra's algorithm (1959) on the tree, or A* with the zero heuristic (Hart, Nilsson, and Raphael 1968). Our implementation adds children lazily: removing a node pushes only its best child and its next sibling, so the frontier stays small though a trigram prefix has $3{,}426$ children, the vocabulary's $3{,}427$ types less the unknown-word token, which we never emit. The argument survives because a sibling not yet pushed is never cheaper than the one standing in for it. The harness checks the search against brute-force enumeration on $200$ random trees, and the work bound on each.

<TheoremBlock type="corollary" number={1} title="Markov models recombine">

In an order-$n$ Markov model, $p(\cdot \mid x, h)$ depends only on the state $\sigma(h)$, the last $n - 1$ tokens. If $\sigma(h) = \sigma(h')$ and $c(h) \le c(h')$, then $h'$ can be discarded, and uniform-cost search becomes Dijkstra's algorithm on a graph of at most $(|V| + 1)^{n - 1}$ states. Exact decoding is polynomial in the size of that graph.

</TheoremBlock>

<TheoremBlock type="proof">

Every completion $z$ has $c(hz) - c(h'z) = c(h) - c(h') \le 0$, because the two prefixes assign $z$ the same conditional probabilities. So any string through $h'$ is matched or beaten by the same string through $h$, and the search may expand each state once.

</TheoremBlock>

The length-layered form of the same dynamic program is Viterbi's algorithm (1967). For the trigram models of the [n-gram topic](/topics/n-gram-models-smoothing) the state is the last two words. With $|V| = 3{,}427$ the corollary's bound is $3{,}428^2$ states, and it is only a bound: a context never seen in training backs off to its last word alone, so past the start of a sentence our trigram has at most $17{,}149$ states. On our $188$ prompts, which are introduced below, Dijkstra reaches the exact mode after a median of $8$ removals and at most $114$, and a search without recombination returns the same string every time.

For a neural network nothing recombines: two prefixes never have the same future. Chen and colleagues (2018) show how hard the problem then is. For a single-layer ReLU recurrent network with rational weights, deciding whether some string has probability above a threshold is undecidable (their Theorem 8). For a consistent network it is decidable, but the best string can be longer than any computable function of the network's size (their Theorems 9–10). Restricted to strings of polynomial length it is NP-complete (their Theorem 11).

## Greedy and beam search: pruned uniform-cost search

Uniform-cost search is exact but its frontier can grow without bound. The decoders used in practice keep a frontier of fixed size.

<TheoremBlock type="definition" number={2} title="Beam search">

**Beam search** with width $k$ forms, at each step, every child of every open prefix and keeps the $k$ most probable. Kept children that end in $\mathrm{eos}$ move to a finished set; the rest stay open. The search stops once the best finished string is at least as probable as the best open prefix, which no extension can beat. **Greedy decoding** is beam search with $k = 1$.

</TheoremBlock>

The beam idea goes back to Lowerre's speech recognizer (1976). Pruning trades the guarantee of Theorem 1 for a fixed budget, and the trade has no floor.

<TheoremBlock type="theorem" number={2} title="No approximation guarantee">

(a) For every $\varepsilon \gt 0$ there is a model on which greedy decoding returns a string less than $\varepsilon$ times as probable as the mode. (b) For every $k$ there is a model on which beam search with width $k$ misses the mode and width $k + 1$ finds it. (c) Beam search with width $k + 1$ can return a strictly less probable string than width $k$.

</TheoremBlock>

<TheoremBlock type="proof">

(a) The first token is $a$ with probability $0.6$ or $b$ with $0.4$. After $a$ come $m$ equally likely words, each followed by $\mathrm{eos}$; after $b$ comes $\mathrm{eos}$. Greedy decoding takes $a$ and returns probability $0.6/m$, while $b\,\mathrm{eos}$ has $0.4$. The ratio $1.5/m$ is below $\varepsilon$ once $m \gt 1.5/\varepsilon$.

(b) Take $k$ decoys of probability $(1 - p_a)/k$ each and a word $a$ with $p_a = 0.9/(k + 1)$, so each decoy is a little likelier than $a$. Each decoy forks into two words that end the string, and $a$ ends at once. Width $k$ keeps the decoys and returns probability $(1 - p_a)/(2k) \lt p_a$. Width $k + 1$ also keeps $a$, its next candidate $a\,\mathrm{eos}$ outranks every other, and the search stops with the mode.

(c) After $a$ ($0.6$) come ten words of $0.1$, each ending the string. After $b$ ($0.4$) come $c$ and $d$ ($0.5$ each), then fifty words of $0.02$, each ending the string. Greedy decoding returns $0.06$. Width $2$ keeps $a$ and $b$, and at the second step $bc$ and $bd$ ($0.2$ each) push out every $ax_i$ ($0.06$), so it returns $0.004$. Width $3$ keeps one $ax_i$ and returns $0.06$.

</TheoremBlock>

Part (c) is not only a construction. On our two corpus models a wider beam returned a strictly less probable string than a narrower one on $0.016$ of the prompts.

## A certificate

Huang, Zhao, and Ma (2017) stop beam search as soon as the best finished string beats every open prefix, and prove the result optimal "modulo beam size": the best among the strings the beam could have kept (their Theorem 1). One more number turns that into a guarantee about every string.

<TheoremBlock type="theorem" number={3} title="A certificate of optimality">

Let $f$ be beam search's best finished string, $m$ the highest log-probability of any candidate it pruned, and $o$ the highest log-probability of a prefix still open when it stopped. If $\log p(f) \ge \max(m, o)$, then $f$ is a mode.

</TheoremBlock>

<TheoremBlock type="proof">

Take any finished string $y$. At each step its prefix was either kept or pruned, and once kept it is finished or open. So $y$ finished inside the beam, and then $\log p(y) \le \log p(f)$; or it extends a pruned candidate, and then $\log p(y) \le m$; or it extends a prefix still open, and then $\log p(y) \le o$. In the last two cases we use that log-probability never rises along a path.

</TheoremBlock>

The certificate is sufficient, not necessary. It stays silent whenever the beam pruned a prefix more probable than its answer, even if every completion of that prefix turned out worse. In the example of the overview, width $2$ pruned "fell" at $0.20$, below the answer's $0.238$, so its answer is certified. Greedy decoding pruned "was" at $0.35$, above its answer's $0.0864$, so its answer is not.

**Length normalization** ranks finished strings by $\log p(y)/\mathrm{lp}(n)$ instead, where $n = |y| + 1$ is the number of generated tokens including $\mathrm{eos}$ and $|y|$, as in Theorem 1, counts only the words before it. Wu and colleagues (2016) use $\mathrm{lp}(n) = ((5 + n)/6)^\alpha$, and $\alpha = 0$ is plain probability. A longer string can now outscore its own prefix, and both Theorem 1 and Theorem 3 lose their footing. A length cap restores the certificate.

<TheoremBlock type="proposition" number={2} title="The certificate under a length cap">

Suppose strings have at most $T$ tokens, $\mathrm{eos}$ included, and $\mathrm{lp}$ is non-decreasing. If the best finished normalized score is at least $\max(m, o)/\mathrm{lp}(T)$, the result is the normalized optimum among strings of at most $T$ tokens.

</TheoremBlock>

<TheoremBlock type="proof">

A completion $y$ of a prefix $h$, with $n \le T$ tokens, has $\log p(y) \le \log p(h) \le 0$ and $\mathrm{lp}(n) \le \mathrm{lp}(T)$, so $\log p(y)/\mathrm{lp}(n) \le \log p(y)/\mathrm{lp}(T) \le \log p(h)/\mathrm{lp}(T)$. Repeat the three cases of Theorem 3 with this bound.

</TheoremBlock>

The same bound gives the normalized beam a sound place to stop, and our implementation uses it.

## Length bias

Why should the mode be short? Every word costs a factor of at most one, and stopping costs one factor too. Whenever stopping is the single most likely move, nothing after it can catch up.

<TheoremBlock type="theorem" number={4} title="The pull of the end mark">

(a) If $\mathrm{eos}$ is the likeliest next token after a prefix $h$, then no completion of $h$ beats stopping: $\max_z p(hz \mid x) = p(h\,\mathrm{eos} \mid x)$. (b) If every word's step has probability at most $q \lt 1$, then $p(y \mid x) \le q^{|y|}$, so $|y^\star| \le \log p(y' \mid x)/\log q$ for every finished string $y'$.

</TheoremBlock>

<TheoremBlock type="proof">

(a) A completion that starts with a word $w \ne \mathrm{eos}$ has $p(hwz) = p(h)\, p(w \mid h)\, p(z \mid hw) \le p(h)\, p(w \mid h) \le p(h)\, p(\mathrm{eos} \mid h)$. (b) The $|y|$ word steps contribute at most $q^{|y|}$ and the final $\mathrm{eos}$ at most $1$. Then $q^{|y^\star|} \ge p(y^\star) \ge p(y')$, and taking logarithms reverses the inequality because $\log q \lt 0$.

</TheoremBlock>

Part (a) needs only that $\mathrm{eos}$ beats each word separately. With probability $0.1$ against $0.9$ spread over twenty words, it does. On the trigram the end mark is the argmax after only $0.037$ of the prompts, yet the mode is the empty continuation for $0.197$ of them: the condition is sufficient, far from necessary. Murray and Chiang (2018) relate the bias to local normalization, where every step must hand out a full unit of probability, the label bias of locally normalized models.

The usual remedies reward length: divide by $\mathrm{lp}(|y| + 1)$, or add a reward per word. Both break the monotonicity of Proposition 1, and the first can leave nothing to find.

<TheoremBlock type="proposition" number={3} title="Normalization can have no optimum">

Under the model with $p(w) = 0.9$ and $p(\mathrm{eos}) = 0.1$ after every prefix, and with $\alpha = 1$, the string $w^n\,\mathrm{eos}$ has $n$ words and $n + 1$ tokens, so its normalized score is $f(n) = 6\,(n \log 0.9 + \log 0.1)/(n + 6)$. It is strictly increasing in $n$ and tends to $6 \log 0.9$, so no string attains the supremum.

</TheoremBlock>

<TheoremBlock type="proof">

The derivative of $f$ has the sign of $6 \log 0.9 - \log 0.1$, which is positive because $0.1 \lt 0.9^6 \approx 0.531$. A strictly increasing bounded sequence converges to its supremum and never reaches it, formalCalculus' [monotone convergence theorem](https://www.formalcalculus.com/topics/sequences-limits) (its Theorem 1).

</TheoremBlock>

The value $\alpha = 1$ is the edge. For general $\alpha$ the derivative has the sign of $(1 - \alpha)\, n \log 0.9 + 6 \log 0.9 - \alpha \log 0.1$. At $\alpha \lt 1$ the first term is negative and grows without bound, so the chain has an optimum: at $\alpha = 0.5$ it is ten words, scoring $-2.055$. At $\alpha \ge 1$ the sign stays positive, the score rises forever, and no string attains the supremum.

At $\alpha = 1$, a string that keeps repeating a cycle has a normalized score tending to $-6$ times the cycle's mean cost, because $\mathrm{lp}(n)$ grows like $n/6$. The cheapest mean over all cycles is the quantity Karp (1978) characterized, so the limit is a multiple of his number, and only at $\alpha = 1$. Such a limit can beat every finite string, as it does on this chain, though it need not: the finance case study below measures a corpus where it does not. Huang, Zhao, and Ma bound their per-word reward against that possibility, and a cap $T$ does the same job here.

Meister, Cotterell, and Vieira (2020) read the problem the other way round: beam search is the exact solution of a *regularized* decoding objective. Write $u_t(w) = -\log p(w \mid x, y_{\lt t})$ for the surprisal of $w$ at step $t$.

<TheoremBlock type="proposition" number={4} title="Greedy decoding is a regularized optimum">

Let $R(y) = \sum_t \big(u_t(y_t) - \min_w u_t(w)\big)^2$. On a model with finitely many strings and no ties, there is a $\lambda_0 \lt \infty$ such that for every $\lambda \gt \lambda_0$ the unique maximizer of $\log p(y \mid x) - \lambda R(y)$ is the greedy string $g$.

</TheoremBlock>

<TheoremBlock type="proof">

$R(y) \ge 0$, with equality exactly when every step of $y$ takes the likeliest token, which without ties means $y = g$. Set $\lambda_0 = \max_{y \ne g} \big(\log p(y) - \log p(g)\big)/R(y)$, a maximum over finitely many strings. For $\lambda \gt \lambda_0$ and $y \ne g$, $\log p(y) - \lambda R(y) \lt \log p(g) = \log p(g) - \lambda R(g)$.

</TheoremBlock>

This is their Theorem 3.1 in its simplest case, with their regularizer of Eq. 11. Their Theorem 3.2 extends it to width $k$ with a regularizer on sets of $k$ strings, which we cite. The regularizer penalizes each step whose surprisal exceeds the locally best one, a form of **uniform information density**: beam search prefers strings that spread their surprisal evenly, and that preference, not the mode, may be what makes its outputs read well.

## Search error against model error

We now measure both kinds of error on two models trained on the shared filings. The prompts are the $188$ distinct three-word openings of held-out MD&A sentences, from Topic 3's held-out split, whose words are all in both models' vocabularies. The **trigram** is the n-gram topic's interpolated Kneser–Ney model over $3{,}427$ words, exact by Dijkstra on its state graph. The **decoder** is the toy causal transformer of the [scaling-laws topic](/topics/scaling-laws): two pre-LN blocks of width $32$ over the $509$ commonest words, trained by that topic's recipe on the training sentences, and exact by uniform-cost search with no recombination. Both are decoded under a per-step renormalization that never emits the unknown-word token.

<BeamSearchLab client:visible />

On the trigram, greedy decoding misses the mode on $0.803$ of the prompts. That count includes the $53$ of $188$ on which it never finishes, for a reason we come to below; among the $135$ it finishes, it misses on $0.726$. Width $4$ misses on $0.282$, width $16$ on $0.027$, and width $32$ never, with every answer from there on certified by Theorem 3. Meanwhile the outputs shrink. A string that never ends has no length, so compare on greedy's $135$ prompts: $4.504$ words under greedy decoding, $2.933$ at width $4$, $2.526$ at width $16$, and $2.415$ for the mode. Over all $188$, width $16$ gives $2.447$ words and width $32$ reaches the mode's $2.367$. The sentences the prompts came from continue for $7.298$ words.

The decoder searches harder, since nothing recombines. Its exact search takes a median of $25.5$ removals and at most $4{,}292$, and width $64$ still misses the mode on $0.207$ of the prompts. That rate belongs to one training seed, the hardest of the four we tried. Seeds $1$ to $3$ miss on $0.074$, $0.059$, and $0.059$ at width $64$. On our seed the rate falls to $0.037$ at width $256$ and to $0$ at width $1{,}024$. Its mode is never empty, though seeds $1$ to $3$ have $4$, $2$, and $1$ empty modes. At $4.862$ words ($4.532$, $4.691$, and $4.803$ on the other seeds) the mode too is shorter than the text, and our seed's outputs shorten from $6.665$ words to $5.511$ as the beam widens.

The strings get shorter, but whether they get blander depends on how we count. On greedy's $135$ prompts, the trigram's cost per token averaged by prompt rises with width: $1.082$ nats under greedy decoding, $1.278$ at width $4$, $1.326$ at width $16$, and $1.393$ for the mode. Pooled over tokens it falls, from $0.982$ to $0.820$. The words themselves get much cheaper, from $1.001$ nats to $0.267$, while the final $\mathrm{eos}$ gets dearer, from $0.900$ to $2.156$, and the average by prompt gives a short string, where that $\mathrm{eos}$ weighs most, the same vote as a long one. The mode is blander than the text by either count, as the case study below shows. Against greedy decoding, all we can say is that it is shorter.

So a wider beam is a better search for a shorter answer. Koehn and Knowles (2017) found the same in translation: quality falls beyond a moderate width, mostly because translations get shorter. Stahlberg and Byrne (2019) ran exact search on a Transformer translation model and found that the empty translation was the mode for $51.8\%$ of their test sentences. Cohen and Beck (2019) traced the damage to early discrepancies: wide beams find strings that pay for one unlikely token early and then follow a highly probable path. The fault is the model's, not the search's, and Eikema and Aziz (2020) argue that the mode is the wrong summary of a spread-out distribution in any case. Ours are spread out: the median mode has probability $0.065$ on the trigram and $0.024$ on the decoder.

## Non-termination

Search error and model error both assume the search ends. It need not.

<TheoremBlock type="theorem" number={5} title="Greedy decoding is eventually periodic">

On an order-$n$ Markov model with state set $S$, $|S| \le (|V| + 1)^{n - 1}$, greedy decoding either emits $\mathrm{eos}$ within $|S|$ steps or never does.

</TheoremBlock>

<TheoremBlock type="proof">

Greedy decoding's next token is a function of the state, so the states follow a deterministic map $\sigma_{t+1} = F(\sigma_t)$ on the finite set $S$. Among $\sigma_0, \dots, \sigma_{|S|}$ two coincide, say $\sigma_i = \sigma_j$ with $i \lt j$, and from then on the states, and with them the tokens, repeat with period $j - i$. If $\mathrm{eos}$ has not appeared by step $j$, it never will.

</TheoremBlock>

On the trigram, greedy decoding cycles on $0.282$ of the prompts, and every cycle is the same one. After "include" the likeliest next word is "and", and after "and and" it is "and" again. The prompts reach it by different roads, such as "analysts who cover halvorsen industrial reported revenue of million for the year include and and and", and only $27$ of the $53$ pass through "include". It looks like the repetition that Holtzman and colleagues (2020) observed when neural models are decoded by maximization, but here the cause is smoothing. Neither "include and" nor "and and" occurs in training. With no count to go on, Kneser–Ney backoff weighs a word by how many distinct words it follows, and "and" follows $1{,}224$, the most of any type ($\mathrm{eos}$ is second at $1{,}019$). The unseen context "and and" falls back on the bigram row of "and", where "and" gets only that backoff share, and it still edges out "markets", a word seen there, $0.0283$ to $0.0276$. So the loop is a smoothing artifact, decided by that narrow margin.

A neural model is not finite-state, but consistency does not rescue it.

<TheoremBlock type="proposition" number={5} title="A consistent model on which beam search never ends">

Take $k$ words of probability $(1 - \varepsilon)/k$ each and $\mathrm{eos}$ of probability $\varepsilon \lt (1 - \varepsilon)/k$, the same after every prefix. The model is consistent, and beam search with width $k$ never terminates.

</TheoremBlock>

<TheoremBlock type="proof">

$\Pr(|Y| \ge t) = (1 - \varepsilon)^t \to 0$, so the model is consistent. At every step, the $k$ word-children of the best open prefix score above every $\mathrm{eos}$-child of any open prefix, so no finished string is ever kept.

</TheoremBlock>

Welleck and colleagues (2020) prove the general version: for every decoder that considers fewer than all tokens at each step, there is a consistent recurrent model on which it produces an infinite string with probability one (their Theorem 3.4). Their remedy is a model whose end-mark probability is forced to grow, under which greedy decoding and beam search provably end (their Theorems 4.2–4.3).

<FinanceCaseStudy title="Finance case study: the mode is boilerplate">

A filing is written to be predictable: the same disclosures, the same hedges, the same sentence frames. A decoder that maximizes probability finds the most predictable text there is, and on the filings that text is boilerplate or nothing at all.

**The mode stops.** The trigram's exact mode is the empty continuation for $0.197$ of the MD&A prompts, and averages $2.367$ words where the real sentences continue for $7.298$. What it does say is cheap. Averaged by prompt it costs $1.367$ nats per word, against $2.278$ for the held-out sentences themselves under the same model. (That $2.278$ covers the $182$ prompts with a held-out sentence wholly in the vocabulary, where the mode costs $1.344$.) Pooled over tokens, it costs $0.835$ against $2.576$. A model asked for the most probable continuation of "benedikt kraus chief" answers "executive", and stops.

**Normalization finds the template.** Dividing by Wu and colleagues' length penalty with $\alpha = 1$ lengthens the trigram's beam outputs to $7.053$ words at width $64$, close to the text's length, at $0.707$ nats per word by prompt ($0.551$ pooled), blander still. The extra words come from the corpus's cheapest long path. At width $16$, $0.133$ of the prompts end in the same five words, "to disclose a material event", the tail of the 8-K sentence "is filing this current report to disclose a material event". "Brightwater analytics competes" becomes "brightwater analytics competes with ostrander machine works is filing this current report to disclose a material event". A wider beam goes further: that tail takes $0.138$ of the prompts at width $32$ and $0.207$ at width $64$, and a second template, ending "line with the prior period", takes $0.096$, $0.106$, and $0.144$ at widths $16$, $32$, and $64$. Length normalization rewards length, and the longest cheap path in a filing corpus is legal boilerplate.

Proposition 3 is about one chain, and this corpus is not that chain. Across the trigram's whole state graph, the cheapest cycle that avoids $\mathrm{eos}$ costs $0.632$ nats a token: the fourteen-word loop "reported revenue of million for the kestrel turbine services a subsidiary of halvorsen industrial". (The search covers all $17{,}149$ states and each state's $300$ likeliest words, and its potentials prove that the words it cut cannot matter.) Repeating that loop would tend to a score of $-3.79$, below every prompt's normalized score at width $64$, the worst of which is $-3.65$. So on this corpus the objective has an optimum on every prompt, on a string under $364$ tokens long. Raising the cap from $40$ to $80$ or $160$ changes no output at width $16$.

**The certificate goes quiet.** Under $\alpha = 1$ the length-capped certificate of Proposition 2 fires on only $0.261$ of the prompts at width $64$, against all of them at $\alpha = 0$. That share depends on the cap. It is $0.431$ at $T = 20$, $0.261$ at $T = 40$, and $0.043$ at $T = 80$, and each row certifies over a different set, the strings of at most $T$ tokens. A normalized objective is harder to search and harder to verify, and on this corpus it trades an empty answer for a templated one. What an analyst wants from a model of filings, the sentence that carries the quarter's news, is in neither the mode nor its normalized cousin. The next topic samples instead of maximizing.

</FinanceCaseStudy>

## Where this goes next

The sampling topic, next in this track, gives up on the mode. Temperature, top-$k$, and nucleus sampling draw strings in proportion to (a reshaped) probability, and the gap between the mode's $1.367$ nats per word and the text's $2.278$, both averaged by prompt, is the typicality question it takes up: real text is not the most probable text. The BLEU and ROUGE topic supplies what this page measured only by length, a score of the output against a reference, and it is on such scores that the beam-search curse was first seen.

Three things are outside our scope. Minimum Bayes risk decoding, which Eikema and Aziz advocate, picks the string with the highest expected similarity to the model's samples rather than the most probable one. Constrained, diverse, and speculative decoding change what is searched for or how fast. And heuristic search with learned estimates of the remaining cost, A* proper, needs a bound on the future that language models do not supply.

## Honest caveats

<RigorFlag>

1. **Proved and cited.** Theorems 1–5 and Propositions 1–5 are proved here. The hardness of exact decoding for recurrent networks, Meister and colleagues' regularizer for $k \gt 1$, and Welleck and colleagues' recurrent construction are cited; Propositions 4 and 5 prove only their simplest cases.
2. **Small models.** The trigram has $3{,}427$ words and the decoder $509$, with two blocks of width $32$. Search-error rates at a given width depend on the model, the prompts, the length cap, and the training seed: at width $64$ the decoder misses on $0.207$ of the prompts on our seed and on $0.059$ to $0.074$ on three others. They do not transfer to large models.
3. **A renormalized model.** Both engines are decoded conditioned, step by step, on never emitting the unknown-word token. That is not the model that was trained, nor that model conditioned on the event globally.
4. **A narrow notion of model error.** We measure length and per-word predictability against held-out synthetic text, not human judgment and not BLEU.
5. **No exact normalized optimum on the corpus.** Under length normalization the page reports beam search and the share of prompts on which Proposition 2 certifies it, a lower bound on how often it found the optimum.
6. **Synthetic filings.** The prompts and the boilerplate come from the shared generator's templates, which make the corpus more repetitive than real filings.

</RigorFlag>

## Implementation

The companion notebook (`notebookPath`) imports `beam_search_decoding.py`, which owns every number on this page. That module imports the n-gram topic's module for the Kneser–Ney trigram, the perplexity topic's module for its vectorized next-word distributions, and the scaling-laws topic's module for the causal decoder and its gradient, which it trains from scratch in NumPy for $1{,}200$ steps. It implements uniform-cost search with lazy successors and optional recombination, beam search that runs several length penalties in one pass and reports Theorem 3's certificate, greedy orbits on finite-state models, and a cheapest-cycle search with its proof of optimality. A harness of $26$ assertion-based tests runs in about two and a half minutes on a laptop CPU, most of it the three extra decoder seeds and the widest beams. The tests cover:

- Theorem 1 against brute-force enumeration on $200$ random trees, with the work bound on each, the inconsistent model on which it fails, a consistent model with $\mathbb{E}|Y| = \infty$, and graph search against tree search on every trigram prompt.
- Theorem 2's three constructions, for $\varepsilon$ down to $10^{-3}$ and widths $1$ to $8$, and non-monotone widths on both corpus models.
- Theorem 3's soundness on $100$ random trees and on both corpus models at every width, and Proposition 2's on the normalized runs.
- Theorem 4 on every prefix and every string of $100$ random trees, Proposition 3's monotone limit and the chain's optimum at $\alpha = 0.5$, and Proposition 4 on $50$ trees.
- Greedy decoding's lengths and misses on the $135$ prompts it finishes, and the decoder's search numbers on four seeds and at widths $256$ and $1{,}024$.
- Every decoder score against one full forward pass of the decoded string, to $10^{-9}$ in double precision.
- Theorem 5 on every trigram prompt, the backoff counts behind the loop, and Proposition 5 for widths $1$ to $5$.
- The cheapest-cycle search against Karp's formula on $50$ random digraphs, its $0.632$ against the trigram itself, the template shares, and the certificate against the cap.
